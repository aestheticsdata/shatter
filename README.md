# SHATTER

Amiga-style brick breaker for one player. Originally written in 2007, rebuilt in 2026 from the playable Claude Design mockup ([SHATTER.dc.html](https://claude.ai/design/p/ef3c88b2-5b26-4718-81f4-0d354e0592bc?file=SHATTER.dc.html)) — REV 2.0.

The game runs on a fixed **480×300 stage** scaled to fit the viewport: a **372×300 canvas playfield** (pixel-art rendering) next to a **108px Workbench-style side panel** (DOM). Vite + strict TypeScript + native CSS with Lightning CSS.

How it is built — the loop, the module graph, the capsule pipeline, all drawn in ASCII — is in [docs/ARCHITECTURE.md](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md).

## Gameplay

- **Screens**: title (animated copper bars) → serve → play, with pause, level-clear, game-over, and a hall of fame with 3-letter initials entry. `L` from the title opens the **LEVELS gallery**: every layout in the roster as a live-rendered miniature of the real field — six a page, `←`/`→` to page, and nothing exported, so a new level appears there with no second edit. `B` opens the **CAPSULES catalogue** on the same scaffolding: the whole roster sorted by rarity, each entry a staged miniature of the effect on the field, drawn by the game's own sprites, with the pill at real size and the registry's one-line `blurb` under it.
- **38 levels**: SUNRISE, SMILEY, PYRAMID, CHOMP, GATEWAY, HEART, VORTEX, BOLT, CHECKER, INVADER, RAMPART, ROCKET, HELIX, TETRA, ORBIT, COOL, HIVE, DNA, SERPENT, SKULL, MIRROR, BUNKER, CASCADE, PLAY, MAZE, OMEGA, 1991, PILLARS, GALAXY, SUPER MAZE, PONG, PACHINKO, KEYHOLE, EYE, HOURGLASS, CENTIPEDE, FLOPPY, FINALE — looping with increasing ball speed. Silver bricks take 2 hits, gold bricks 3, and granite 4 — or two laser bolts, which is how SUPER MAZE is meant to be got through: it is a labyrinth of nothing but granite, with two LASER capsules pinned to fixed bricks so every run is handed the cannons.
- **Per-level backgrounds**: eight playfield themes (starfield, nebula haze, blueprint grid, sunrise horizon, gas giant, circuit board, CRT cathode, stone vault), assigned so no two consecutive levels look alike, each seeded per level so the levels sharing a theme still differ. Every theme is static and stays darker than the sprite palette — a `check:backgrounds` script enforces it.
- **Power-ups** dropped by destroyed bricks, each catch acknowledged by a floating label at the paddle. How often a brick drops anything at all is `bonusSpreadAmount`; _which_ capsule it drops is drawn from a shuffled bag rather than rolled against weights, and the two are independent. The rarity tier below buys tickets in that bag — two for a common, one for everything else, plus DEMAKE and GIANT which keep a common's two — so a pass is 74 tickets and every capsule in the roster falls once or twice inside it. Nothing can go missing for longer than two passes, roughly six levels on average and twelve at the worst; a `check:drops` script enforces it. One capsule is not left to the bag at all: **the first level always holds a DEMAKE**, seeded into its wall rather than rolled for.

  **FENCE is the only capsule that builds something on your half of the field.** Every other trap takes something away — the deck's width, the wall's solidity, the ball's line. This _adds_: for ten seconds six one-hit posts stand on grid row 16, y 230–242, which is a short hop over the rail at 276, and the empty band the ball has crossed for nothing since 1986 now has masonry in the bottom of it. A ball leaving your deck meets a post in a few ticks and comes straight back at you, and every shot you want at the real wall has to be threaded through one of the five 30 px gaps. **The 34 px of air under it is the one number that may not be shaved.** Row 17 is tighter and reads better at 22 px, and GIANT's ball is 24 — a player holding both would have a ball that could not pass under its own fence. **A post pays nothing and holds nothing**: 0 points, no capsule, so PAYDAY and TURBO have nothing here to multiply and XRAY correctly shows the posts empty. They are **not counted toward the clear** either, which cuts both ways — a fence standing when the wall empties cannot hold the level open, and a fence expiring on its own cannot clear it for you. Bricks you break stay broken, so smashing a hole early is the skill and waiting it out is the safe play; the real counterplay is a LASER, and a live CHAIN will take the whole row for nothing, since the posts sit exactly two columns apart and that is an arc's shortest jump. **The posts are wall cells and they are not the wall**, and that split is the whole design: everything that finds a brick by _pixel_ — the ball, a bolt, a meteor — meets the fence with no new branch, while everything that walks the wall by _index_ goes on meaning the wall, so QUAKE cannot slide it, SLUMP and JELLY cannot move it, PAYDAY's gild front cannot reach it and `remaining` never counts it. GHOST does take it intangible along with everything else, which is consistent and is a mercy. **It arrives driven in and leaves pulled out.** Each post telescopes down out of the top of its cell over eight ticks with a puff of dust as it seats, staggered left to right so a fence visibly goes up across your own half of the field in about four tenths of a second; at expiry the survivors snap _up_ out of their seats from the ends inward, the middle pair last, each bursting into the ordinary chunk debris — and the pull is armed seventeen ticks early, so the last post bursts on the tick the capsule ends rather than leaving a fence standing after its timer. **A second FENCE repairs rather than doubles**: the phase is kept and only the posts you have already smashed are driven back in, because twelve posts in a row would be a wall and the gaps are the capsule. The posts wear **green-stained timber** against eight bodies of masonry, which is the whole job of those three tones — they have to read as _not your wall_ at the moment they arrive, while the ball is still in the air.

  **TWIN is the only capsule in the roster that pays out across the field.** For eighteen seconds the wall pairs itself up: twelve couples drawn at random among the live bricks, each joined by a shivering hairline that can cross the whole grid, and **a hit on either half is paid by both on the same tick, wherever the partner is standing**. It is not a destroy — a granite partner chips exactly as the struck brick chips, and the thread stays up until one of them actually dies. The payoff is the _distance_: most draws are unremarkable neighbours, and every so often the shuffle threads the bottom-left corner to the top-right and one ordinary shot takes a brick you had no angle on. Because the pairing is redrawn on every catch and topped back up to twelve every second and a half, it is never the same wall twice and it does not spend itself in the opening rally. **One hop, never a cascade** — a brick belongs to at most one couple, so a partner has no pairing of its own to fire and there is nothing to guard against. **Not CHAIN**: a chain arc is discovered at the moment of a kill and lives for a few frames, while a thread is _declared up front_ and stands on the field for eighteen seconds, so the player reads the wall's wiring before choosing a shot. **The partner is paid by whatever did the killing**, because the link is routed at the grid write rather than at the ball — a NUKE, a ZAP sweep, a grub's bite, a meteor, a PYRE crater and a BLAST splash all take both halves of every couple they touch, and a PIERCE run through the wall can take four bricks out of two couples in one pass. It pays a partner the way every other indirect kill in the game pays: **full points, and no capsule roll** — a seeded drop still comes out, because a seeded drop is the level's promise. The threads are the **held cue** as much as the picture, since the capsule is armed and idle for most of its life: they **grow in from both ends at once and meet in the middle**, which is a link being declared rather than thrown, and at expiry they **go slack, bow out of their anchors and fall through the field**, fading as they drop. A couple broken mid-life does not fade — it snaps, and two hot heads run out along the line from each end so the discharge is seen going both ways. They are drawn as spaced pixels under a travelling wave rather than as solid rules, which keeps them from competing with the ball and is also what survives DEMAKE, where every tone on the field flattens to one ink. Under a BLACKOUT they go dark with everything else and the torch finds them near the ball, which is the honest answer: the carve-out that lights capsules through the veil exists so a trap cannot be caught blind, and there is no such thing as a thread you regret not seeing.

  **LEAP is the only capsule that moves the ball without touching its heading or its speed.** Every 75-105 ticks — rolled fresh each time, so the cadence never becomes a beat you can count against — the ball blinks out and reappears 20-32 px further along the line it was already on, having crossed the gap **without being anywhere in between**. Nothing in that gap is hit: sometimes it skips a granite you were dreading, mostly it skips the brick you had lined up or puts the ball a body-width past where your deck was going to be. **It can never cross the death line.** A jump that would end below the deck is cut short of it, and the frame is cut the same way — the ball may be teleported _past_ your deck sideways, which is the joke, but never into the gutter, because a trap that takes a life with no rally to play is a bug with a blurb on it. It never lands inside anything either: the span walks back toward the ball until it finds a free position, and if there is none the jump simply does not happen. **It never fires inside 20 ticks of a serve**, so a launch is always yours, and a ball held by STASIS or stuck under GLUE does not jump at all — held is not loose. The **held cue is a faint magenta lozenge** hanging on the heading at the exact point the next jump lands, up the whole time: it is the same value the jump spends, so you are shown where the ball is going and then it goes there. Without it the capsule reads as the game dropping frames, which is the failure this roster keeps relearning. It arrives with the ball **going unsure** — twelve ticks of the sprite flickering a pixel either way in place, drawn and never simulated — and it leaves with the jumps **running down**: every span is scaled by the last three seconds, so the final two are about 12 px and 6 px and the lozenge shrinks with them. Each jump leaves a square **closing** where the ball was and one **opening** where it arrived, which is the only thing in the capsule that says which way it went. **TRACER's guide stops at the jump** rather than guessing past it: `THE BALL SHOWS WHERE IT LANDS` cannot survive a tunnel, and recomputing through one would be honest for the first jump and invented for the second.

  **HEISEN is the game's only trade you make several times a rally, and the only multiplier that belongs to a ball rather than to the field.** The longer you leave a ball alone the less precisely the machine draws it — up to four extra copies of its own sprite, drifting inside 5 px, thickening over three seconds — and the more it pays: every brick that ball kills scores at **x1 rising to x3** with its own vagueness. A click observes every live ball at once: the scatter collapses, the multiplier drops back to x1, and it starts climbing again immediately. Clicks are free and unlimited, so the only cost is ever the points you just gave up. **You can know where the ball is, or you can be paid; not both.** It multiplies with PAYDAY and TURBO rather than capping under them — every multiplier here is an independent timer and they have stacked since PAYDAY met TURBO, and TURBO making the balls faster is exactly when the trade should be worth most. Because it is per ball, a SWARM is a dozen different multipliers in one rally. **The ball sprite is never reshaped**: every copy is the sprite as drawn, at 1:1, moved, and the true one is painted last and at full strength, so the ball is crowded and never lost — GIANT's rule, which generates a bigger sprite rather than scaling the drawn one. It is the fourth thing a click can mean and **deliberately the last**: a release wins, then a PYRE fuse, and this takes the click only when neither wanted it. A STASIS-held ball accumulates nothing, being observed by definition. It arrives by **going out of focus** over 15 ticks and leaves by **coming back into it** over 12, the copies converging on the sprite they came out of while the multiplier eases back to x1 across the same window.

  **UMBRA is the only capsule that makes the empty band do anything.** For twenty seconds a sun crosses the top frame from x −60 to x 432, hung 240 px above it, and **the lowest live brick in each column throws a shadow you can hit**: a wedge from that brick's own collider bottom edge, along the real ray from the sun, 96 px straight down. A ball that touches one rebounds off its face and takes a hit point off the brick that cast it, a bright band runs back up the wedge and the caster flashes as it arrives — so the first contact teaches the whole capsule — and the wall can be worked from 96 px below itself, a silver or a gold ground down without the ball ever reaching the grid. Measured over twenty-four scripted rallies on the same headings, a shadow field takes **367 bricks off the wall against 143 without it**, and leaves the ball out of the deck's reach for no longer overall. **One caster per column, not every brick**, and the wedges **taper** from the brick's full 30 px to 12: those two together are the picture. Shadows from every live brick overlap into a solid black slab, and so do twelve untapered ones on a 30 px pitch — on a fresh wall, where every column's lowest brick is in the same row, they tile the band edge to edge. Tapered, the same twelve leave a V of open field between every pair that widens the further it falls, and what hangs under the wall is a forest rather than a floor. **Killing a brick retracts its shadow on the same frame**: the next brick up takes over as caster, its wedge starts 12 px higher and ends 12 px higher, and the darkness visibly retreats as you play — the field opening up _is_ the reward. The sun's travel sweeps the whole field from hard right to hard left over the twenty seconds, so a lane you found closes while a new one opens. The quad is derived from the **collider rect rather than the cell**, so ERODE's worn bricks throw narrower shadows for nothing. It **arrives as a sunrise**: the left frame lights top to bottom and each wedge unfolds out of the foot of its caster a column at a time, left to right, over thirty ticks — nothing is ever drawn at partial strength, a shadow is black at whatever length it has reached or it is not there yet. It **leaves as a sunset, which is not the arrival backwards**: over the last thirty ticks the shadows do not shorten, they _stretch_ — the mouth leaves the brick, the tip runs out past the cap, the whole quad thins to a twelfth and slides off the bottom of the field, and they stop being surfaces on the first tick of it. **UMBRA and BLACKOUT retire each other on catch**, because there is no honest way to draw a black surface under a black veil and an invisible collider is the one defect this roster has never shipped; whichever is live is sent down through its own sunset rather than switched off, so the light is gone before the dark arrives. **Not BUMPERS**, the other capsule in that band: BUMPERS adds five discs that are their own objects and pay 100 a kick, and this adds no objects at all — every collider is a projection of a brick that already exists, dies when that brick dies, and damages its own caster. The point is the 140 px of dead space between the wall and the deck that the ball has crossed for nothing since 1986.

  **TIDE is the only capsule that moves the paddle.** In forty years of this genre the deck's Y has never changed, and for sixteen seconds this one floods the field to y 180 and **floats it 96 px up into the wall's face**, bobbing 2 px on a slow swell. Below the surface every ball takes an upward push and a horizontal damp: a ball that dives is turned round and sent back out, and **the floor stops existing as a concept**. It is not WALL and it is not ANGEL — both of those catch one ball at a line and are spent doing it. This catches nothing. It removes the region, and it charges you your aim for it: return angles off a bobbing deck are unreliable, the water under it is a soup you cannot steer through, and the wall is close enough that a rebound is back on you in eight ticks. Sixteen seconds of guaranteed survival bought with sixteen seconds of no control, played at point-blank range. **The guarantee is arithmetic rather than a guard**: the fastest the game ever runs is 4.6 px a tick and buoyancy turns that round inside 76 px, against 120 px of water — so nothing checks for the floor, because nothing can reach it. The force is applied outside `ballTimeScale()` on purpose, so it composes with TEMPO, RUSH, TURBO and STASIS for nothing and leaves nothing on a ball to unwind when the water goes. **The deck is never moved by the capsule**: the sea is raised and the deck takes the higher of the rail and the surface, so the lift is _caused_ on screen — the sea crosses 17 px of empty field first, climbs the deck's side, and only then takes it off the rail and carries it the remaining 96 px. It floats with a **2 px draft** rather than balanced on the surface, so the water is genuinely over its flank and foam breaks along it for the whole sixteen seconds, and the bob makes that draft breathe between 1 and 3 px; a deck sitting exactly on the waterline is a paddle on a lift. It leaves the other way: a plughole opens at bottom centre with a ring of dither turning around it, the surface **sags over it** as it falls so the sea is visibly going somewhere rather than being lowered, and the deck sets back on the rail and sheds three streaks off its caps. Capsules that fall in sink at a third of their speed and ride the swell, so a pill the deck just missed crosses its catch band in 35 ticks instead of 11 and can still be swept up. The cost of it was one constant: **`gameConfig.paddle.y` stopped being where the deck is and became only where the rail is**, and thirty read sites across the game moved onto deck-owned state.

  **JELLY is the only capsule that takes your kills away and gives you the wall instead.** For twenty seconds the wall stops being a wall and becomes a damped elastic sheet **pinned at its frame**: hitting a brick no longer breaks it, it presses a **6 px dimple** into the sheet, and the ball comes off the trampoline **1.35× faster for that bounce**. The dimple rolls outward at a cell every two ticks, reflects off both side frames, and re-crosses its own reflection about half a second after the ball landed. **The damage is the crossing.** A cell earns strain from how far it is bent past a pixel and a bit and gives it back every tick regardless, so a single front passing is worth almost nothing — but where two fronts meet the displacements add, the strain spikes, and after three of them the brick bursts, in a place the ball never touched and at a position that is a function of where it struck. One hit loads a dozen cells and kills none of them; a rally of five or six contacts opens between four and twenty, and the spread between those two numbers is the whole skill: a rally that happens to drive the sheet near its own period gets the twenty. A tear goes through **the ordinary damage path**, so it pays points, drops the capsule the brick was holding, splashes a live BLAST and links a live CHAIN exactly as a ball's own kill does — and it is a _hit_, so granite still takes four. **The load is drawn on the bricks' own authored tones**: a charging cell steps down its damage ramp exactly as a hit one does, so you watch the wall go dark before it tears, in art that already existed. The five one-hit bricks get one extra tone for it — their own `dark`, the shade along their bottom edge, which has never been a body before and is a red brick going maroon on its way out. **It arrives as a rope going slack**: the slack starts at the two pinned ends and runs inward to meet in the middle over twenty ticks, each column ringing down through two overshoots, so you are shown what the wall is made of before you are asked to use it. It leaves the other way — over the last twenty-four ticks the propagation walks to zero and the amplitude ceiling collapses from the outer columns inward, so the edges stiffen first and the centre is the last thing still moving. **A displaced brick never moves into a ball**: a cell with a ball standing in it holds where it is and catches up once the ball has gone, which is ERODE's guard for ERODE's reason — a bounce resolves by walking the ball back its own sub-step, under two pixels, and a brick that came six would leave it inside. The hitbox follows the paint per cell rather than per wall, which is the one place this capsule cost the engine something real: `cellAt` stopped being a division and became a search over the rows within the sheet's reach. A **laser bolt is deliberately exempt** and still drills stone while the wall is pudding — the capsule is about what a _ball_ does to a sheet, and a player holding a LASER over a JELLY has earned the only way left to take a brick down on demand.

  **SLUMP is the only capsule whose effect is a picture of the damage you already did.** For sixteen seconds the wall has gravity: every brick falls until it lands on the brick below it or on the **slump floor** — row eleven, y 170, which is three rows below the deepest wall in the game — and it stays where it lands for the rest of the level. **Nothing dies from falling.** A slumped wall is the same bricks, closer, and the score is untouched. Because the floor sits under every wall rather than inside it, the catch **always has a first collapse**: even a fresh level with no holes in it comes down four to seven rows in about three quarters of a second. On a wall you have been eating, the columns compact into their own gaps on the way, and what you are left with is a **ragged skyline** — every column's bottom on the same floor and its top wherever its own losses left it, the flat band you built up now a profile of where you have been hitting. The bricks keep their colours as they stack, so a column that lost its yellow reads red over orange over green and you can see they are the same bricks. Gravity then **stays on**: every brick you kill for the rest of the sixteen seconds drops the column above it onto the gap, so the wall goes on eating itself downward while you play. **Not QUAKE**, which is instant, rigid and uniform — a row vaporised and the whole wall shifted 12 px as one body. This kills nothing, is per column, and runs for sixteen seconds; in code QUAKE's `dropOffset` is one number and this is ninety-six that disagree. **It arrives as a hesitation**: for four ticks every brick's bottom bevel goes to its own sheen — the shadow it was casting on the brick under it going away — and the wall visibly un-seats itself before a pixel of it moves. It leaves the other way up: over the last half second a line of pale mortar runs from the floor to the top row, cell by cell, each locking as the line passes. The fall went down, the setting goes up. **The grid is never touched, only the pixels** — a brick keeps its cell and gains an offset, which is what stops the fall dragging ERODE's wear, JELLY's strain, each brick's held capsule and granite's seed along with it, and what keeps BUMPERS' band, METEOR's floor and HOMING's "below the wall" line where every other level in the game has them. A landing squeezes dust out of both sides of the cell it hit, scaled by how hard it came down.

  **GAMBLE hands the decision to a machine.** Catching it opens a framed reel over your deck that turns ten faces in a second, lands on one, holds it for a fifth of a second, and then fires it — a NUKE, a SWARM, an extra life, anything on the roster **except itself and except the traps**. A lottery you cannot stop may not punish you for playing it: catching a capsule that shrinks your deck is a decision you made, and the same thing arriving out of a drum you had no hand in is only the game taking a turn against you. The exclusion is the `trap` tier itself, so it keeps itself current as traps are invented — with **one named exception, DEMAKE**, which stays on the drum because it is the trap that costs nothing but nerve: the machine drops to a 1-bit tube and the game underneath is untouched. BLACKOUT and FLIP are presentation-only in the same way and are not on it; both genuinely take the ball away from you while they last. The faces are **uniform over what is left**, not proportional to how often each capsule drops: the bag's tickets say how often a capsule _falls_, and the point of the reel is that the rare things are on the table. With 38 faces that is 2.6 % each against a rare capsule's 1.7 % of a draw, so the drum runs about 1.6× a rare's drop rate — and about 0.8× a common's, which turns up here _less_ often than it falls. Both ratios move every time a capsule is invented. The reel is drawn on the field rather than in the POWER inset, because the reel _is_ the effect and a player watching the panel would miss the one second it exists — and it is the one capsule with no catch pop, since the label and the drum would print over each other. A spin still turning when the level clears or the ball drains dies with the rest of the run state.

  **ANGEL is the one thing in a run that outlives the ball that bought it.** Caught, it arms a single save and **your deck grows wings** — two silver tufts that stir on the ends of the paddle for as long as you are holding one, because a capsule you cannot see yourself holding is a capsule that appears to do nothing until the minute it fires. The next ball that crosses the death line is thrown back above it in a burst of the same feathers with a `SAVED` pop, the wings go, and the life you were about to spend is never spent — it will void a game over on your last life. It only ever fires on the **last ball on the field**, because a charge burned while eleven others are still in flight is a charge nobody saw being spent, and WALL never has to be arbitrated against it: the barrier sits at 294 and turns the ball around six pixels above where ANGEL is even asked. Every other piece of run state is wiped by the serve after a lost ball; the charge deliberately is not, which is what carries it from one level to the next. A new run and a game over clear it.

  **RUSH and TURBO are the same knob pulled by opposite hands** — a trap at ×1.8 for five seconds against a rare bonus at ×1.5 for twenty that also triples every brick — so the game never leaves it to the pill you caught to say which is in hand: the smear behind the ball runs **hot for the trap and cold for the bonus**, and the SCORE readout blinks while points are worth more than they say (PAYDAY's own tell, shared). TURBO winds up to speed over half a second rather than arriving, and its comet grows out of the ball as it does. Caught together they multiply — 4.6 × 1.8 × 1.5 is 12.4 px a tick, seven sub-steps, and the wall still stops the ball — because a bonus that quietly did nothing under a trap would read as a broken capsule. The ×3 is brick kills only: the level-clear bonus and a bumper kick take PAYDAY alone, or FINALE would pay 42 000 and a ball parked between two discs would farm 300 a kick.

  **The deck telescopes rather than jumping between widths.** WIDE, XWIDE, JAMMER and SPLIT all drive the same two rigid end caps the sprite is already built from: the caps travel one pixel each per tick and the body fills in behind them, so WIDE runs out over 13 ticks and XWIDE over 49 — still going long after WIDE would have stopped, which is the capsule saying what it is. It stays anchored on its centre throughout, because the mouse is the only steering and a still hand would otherwise find the deck had grown out from under it; against a wall the near end stays pinned and the deck extrudes into the field instead. **JAMMER is the one that does not run — it snaps**, fast-first over 8 ticks, wearing its own magenta on both caps for exactly as long as it is shutting and leaving the vacated rail marked in a magenta that dies out over the next few seconds. What the deck catches is what the deck is drawn as, all the way through the travel: a 72 px paddle that caught like a 46 px one would be a far worse lie than a ramp.

  **SPLIT tears the deck open rather than teleporting a hole into it.** The catch throws a spray of the paddle's own blue and red off a hairline down its middle, and the halves pull apart over the next ten ticks while the deck is still telescoping out to its split width. The hole is not a hole until it is wider than the 8 px ball, which is about six ticks in — so the crack is visible, and steerable away from, before it can cost anything. The gap is derived from the width rather than eased on a clock of its own: what SPLIT really promises is 20 px of wood either side, and the hole is whatever the deck has left over, capped at the 26 px playtest actually measured. That is what makes it compose — a JAMMER caught over a live SPLIT shuts the deck until there is no room left for a hole and the halves simply meet, and nothing anywhere needs to know that the two mechanisms were running at once. At the far end the halves come back together and **weld**: the seam flashes the deck's own highlight for two ticks, over `splitPickup` run the other way up. Before, the deck healing had no picture and no sound at all — the width branch snapped 66 back to 46 and the hole stopped existing.

  **MIRROR's reflection resolves onto the ceiling rather than switching on.** Frame one is a six-pixel line at the mirrored centre in the ghost's own muted blue; over the next fifth of a second it opens left and right to the full span while the band unfolds upward from a single row, the dim reflection tones filling in behind the leading line and the red caps arriving once there is room for them. **The span it is drawn at is the span it returns balls off** — one function, called by the bounce test and the renderer both — so a ball aimed at where the ghost will be goes straight past it, instead of coming back off a full-width surface nobody could see. The unfold anchors on the bottom edge, because that is the edge an upward ball actually meets. SPLIT's hole scales with it, so the reflection is a shrunken copy of the deck and never a window onto a full-sized one. Departure runs the whole thing backwards and then leaves the line on the ceiling for six more ticks, walking down three tones — an after-image that bounces nothing, which is the only thing on screen explaining the ball that just went through where the ghost was.

  **LASER grows the cannons the roster already promises.** The catch frame stays a bare deck; over the ten ticks before the first bolt each 2×3 stud is extruded out of the deck's top edge, base welded into the housing and only the muzzle climbing — and the muzzle runs white-hot until the frame it locks out, which is the frame the first shot leaves. The pause before that shot stops being dead air under finished hardware and becomes the hardware arriving. It moves in **thirds of a game pixel**, not whole ones: three whole steps over ten ticks is a pop, and the renderer already draws everything that moves on the backing grid, so a third of a pixel is exactly one device pixel and the extrusion gets nine legible steps. At expiry the guns sink back over the same ten ticks while the last bolt is still climbing the field. None of it touches the simulation — the fire gate, the cadence and the first-shot delay all still read the timer, and a LASER fires the same shots on the same ticks it always has.

  **BOMB blows the deck up out of its own pieces.** One frame of whiteout — the whole pill in its own top sheen, the paddle catching the light of the charge under it — and then eleven of three pieces thrown apart and burning down to nothing, with the debris in the deck's own blue, pale blue and red rather than a generic red. The pieces tile the deck exactly on the frame they are cut, so there is no moment where a different sprite appears: the paddle simply stops being one thing. They are **not a blend** — per-piece position and velocity is per-piece state, and they answer to the same gravity the debris does — but the whiteout and what is left of each piece are, and so is BLACKOUT's deck torch, which dies over the same twelve ticks instead of switching off under a player who was steering by it. MIRROR's ghost leaves on that curve too; a reflection has nothing left to reflect. After the twelfth tick the rail is empty for the remaining thirty-three of the forty-five-tick fuse, and that emptiness is the point — it is the beat that says the life is gone. Deliberately no tumble: the sprite brush is axis-aligned and a rotation would break the pixel grid every other sprite is built on.

  **HAYWIRE is the one ball capsule that is not on that clock, and it is the point of it.** Every other trap on the ball argues about speed — RUSH surges, TEMPO drags, STASIS stops — so the one thing left to take was _direction_. Four times a second the machine throws a fault: each live ball's heading is rotated by up to 34 degrees, its speed untouched, and a shower of hot sparks goes off where the kick landed. It never appears in `ballTimeScale()` and never will, which is what lets it compose with all four of them for free and leaves nothing stored on a ball to unwind when it ends — a heading is simply where it is. **The clock is global and the angle is not**: every ball is kicked on the same tick, because what is being said is that the machine has one fault rather than twelve, and each ball then picks its own direction out of that single event, so a SWARM scatters instead of turning as a flock. **The size of every kick is the blend**, which is the whole of both transitions and the reason the capsule needs no second clock: the first kicks are nudges, the middle of the five seconds is the full cone, and the last few shrink back to nudges as the fault clears — the ball is never switched back to obedience, it just stops being interfered with. Two guards keep it a trap you fight rather than one you watch. A kick that would leave the ball flatter than 0.35 vertical **turns the other way instead**, at the same size, since a ball knocked flat rattles between the walls until the timer runs out and a trap that ends by boring you stopped being one. Unlike HOMING's identical-looking test it cannot simply skip: a skipped kick is a spark shower over a ball that did not move, and the sparks would be lying about the simulation. Turning away always lands back inside the cone — the floor puts the ball at least 20.5 degrees off horizontal and a kick is at most 34.4, so it moves toward vertical and cannot reach the far wall. Clamping to the edge survives only as the fallback for a ball that arrived flat by some other route, a singularity or a bumper rather than the deck, whose widest bounce is 0.498 vertical and clear of this; **clamping was the first cut's only answer and it was wrong for the common case**, because a ball parked exactly on the boundary was re-clamped to the heading it already had and every kick toward flat visibly did nothing. The vertical _sense_ is the one the ball already had: a fault may take your aim, but it may not decide that every ball is now heading for the floor. Caught with HOMING the two are left to fight — the lock re-aims every tick and the fault knocks it off every fifteen, and the ball wanders and still finds bricks, which is the honest sum of the two rather than either winning. **The static is drawn around the ball and never on it**: the arcs sit at or beyond the sprite's own radius, hashed off the frame and held four frames at a time so they crackle instead of shimmering, because the eight-pixel silhouette is the one thing on this field that is never allowed to change — what the player sees is a ball with something wrong around it, which is the truth.

  **ENGLISH is the deliberate inverse of HOMING: the curve is yours, and the game does not help you aim it.** For forty seconds whatever the deck was doing in the tick it struck the ball is banked on that ball as spin, and the spin turns its heading a little every tick after — a Magnus force on something that cannot change speed, which is rotation at constant speed, the same trick `steerBall` and `glitchBall` both do for the third reason. The numbers are set against the flight rather than against the deck: a ball leaves the paddle at 3.1 px a tick and meets the wall roughly 65 ticks later, and a spin decaying at 0.99 a tick is worth about 48 ticks of turning, so the hardest whip the player can throw is a 38-degree banana and nothing steeper. **The spin replaces whatever was there rather than adding to it**, which is what makes a straight-bat return a real move — under the deadzone the deck was tracking the ball instead of whipping at it, so the return goes straight _and_ comes off clean. **It reads the deck's whole travel, skid included**, and not the movement BANANA reads: a ball struck by a deck sliding out from under you is a ball with english on it, and the two numbers are kept apart in the source for exactly that disagreement. The frame **mirrors the spin and takes half**, at the ceiling as well as at the two side walls, because a reflection reverses handedness whichever way the surface lies; a brick **spends it outright**, above the PIERCE branch rather than inside it, since a curve is aimed at a brick and a drill that kept its spin would corkscrew through the wall on one whip. Nothing is stored that has to be unwound, and so **the capsule has no ending to switch off**: a ball still curving when the timer runs out keeps curving until its own spin decays away. What ends on the timer is the _surface_ — the felt, laid across the deck's middle row from its own centre outward and rolled back in the same way, on the one row of seven that is body from cap to cap so that GLUE's resin above it and the deck's shade below are both left alone. MIRROR's ghost wears the cloth, because a reflection of a felted deck is a felted deck, but it cannot put english on anything: the ghost is mirrored across the field and therefore travels the opposite way, so a spin taken off it would fight the hand that threw it. The stored spin is shown **around the ball and never on it** — three flecks orbiting a pixel clear of the sprite's radius, on a phase that is the total heading the spin has actually turned the ball through, so they go round the way it is bending, slow as it decays and stop on the tick it is spent. A clock of their own would be a picture of a curve rather than the curve.

  **SNAP puts the ball on graph paper.** For twenty-four seconds every rebound — off a brick, a wall, a disc, the ghost ceiling, the deck, anything — leaves on the nearest of the four 45-degree diagonals, at exactly the speed it arrived with. It is a **quantiser and not a capsule about pace**: the ball covers the same ground, along one of four headings instead of a continuum of them, and what you buy is that a bank shot can be _called_ before it is taken. **The deck still chooses**: the paddle's fan decides which side of vertical the return leaves on, so hitting the ball left of centre sends it up-left and right of centre up-right — the capsule takes the fine control away and leaves the coarse one, which is the whole trade. A rebound with no lateral component at all is sent toward the middle of the field rather than into the corner it was already nearest. **The hook is one test and not seven calls**: the velocity is read at the top of each sub-step and compared at the foot of it, so everything that can turn a ball is covered by construction and the seventh thing to be invented is covered too. It composes rather than arbitrates — ENGLISH's spin bends the ball off its diagonal between bounces and the next bounce puts it back, HOMING pulls it off and the next bounce puts it back, which is the honest sum of two capsules that disagree. The picture is **paper and marks**: a lattice on the brick's own 12 px pitch dithers up out of the field, cell by cell in a fixed scatter rather than fading, because a machine with two states per pixel resolves a grid the way GHOST dissolves a wall — and it is drawn onto the field art itself, outside QUAKE's shake and FLIP's turn, since a ruler that moved with the wall would be measuring nothing. Every snapped bounce leaves a **right-angle bracket** at the point of contact and dashes down the diagonal the ball has just taken, so the guide is drawn where the ball is _about to be_; they go out furthest-first as the mark dies, retracting toward the corner they came from. Nothing has to be undone when the twenty-four seconds end: the next bounce is simply not snapped, and a ball already flying a diagonal keeps flying it until something turns it.

  **ERODE is GHOST's fantasy, delivered.** GHOST makes the wall intangible for five seconds and the ball sails through it having done nothing; ERODE takes the mortar out instead and leaves every brick standing, worn down inside its own cell until the wall is a lattice with lanes through it. For twenty-four seconds each brick's collider — and its sprite, which is the same rectangle — pulls **five pixels off each side and three off the top and bottom**, so a 30 × 12 cell holds a 20 × 6 brick and the columns are 10 px apart. A ball is 8 px across and collides on four corners inset one, so it genuinely fits: it goes up a lane, clips the brick faces either side of it for 1 damage a touch, and comes back down another one. **The two axes are different numbers because they had to be.** Take the ticket's flat 60 % off both and the lanes come out at 12 px, twice what is needed, while the galleries between rows come out at 4.8 — a channel the player can see and the ball cannot enter. Three is also the largest the vertical wear may be: at four the brick is 4 px tall, thinner than the gap between the ball's own top and bottom corners, and a ball crossing it would pass clean through a brick that is still there. At three it is exactly six, the corner test cannot miss it, and the sub-step loop needs nothing added under TURBO or RUSH because it already caps a sub-step at 2 px an axis. Both are spent in **whole pixels** all the way down, so the brick you bounce off is always the brick on screen. **The ending is per brick, not per wall.** When the twenty-four seconds are up the mortar sets again over a second — but a cell with a ball standing in it does not close, and waits for it to leave before walking back at the same rate. A brick that grew back through a ball would swallow it, and a ball spat out of the inside of a brick on a heading nobody threw is worse than any hole. The picture is grit: mortar-coloured grains hashed off each cell's own coordinates trickle out of the seams while the wear is moving, heaviest in the middle of it and gone at both ends, and they **run back up into the seams** while it sets. Ball and laser bolt alike go down the lanes, because the wall changed shape and a hitbox is not told who is asking.

  **GRAVEL makes every kill a decision.** Catch it and the whole wall takes a cracked dry-stone face — a **fault that arrives along the courses**, left to right with a ragged edge, each brick visibly splitting and letting go a little grit as its turn comes. That is the standing cue, and it means exactly one thing: for twenty-four seconds every brick you kill bursts into **four to six chips** that scatter out of it and fall. Each one the deck catches is 30 points and a puff of granite; each one that reaches the floor is gone. They never touch a ball — they are stone in the air, not colliders — so what a kill hands you is a **greed-versus-survival micro-choice** made while the ball is coming back: the chips want your deck under the wall and the ball wants it wherever the ball is going, and a full brick's worth of gravel is roughly what the brick itself paid. **They are a drop, not debris, and every number follows from that.** The fall is capped at 3.2 px a tick, so a chip leaving the top row takes about a second and a half to arrive and can never step over a 7 px deck between two frames; the horizontal is damped, so a shower lands under the brick that threw it instead of sixty pixels away; and the pool refuses rather than recycles, because the slot being reused would be a chip you had already decided to go for. That pool is also the cap — a NOVA taking twenty-five cells at once asks for a hundred and twenty chips and gets what is left, which is a shower you can read instead of a screen of stone. **Only kills a ball is behind crumble**: direct, laser, BLAST splash and CHAIN link all pay out, while a NUKE, a ZAP, the grub, a meteor and a PYRE crater take the wall without paying it, the same line those five already sit on for capsule drops — thirteen bricks each throwing five 30-point chips would make one spent ball worth more than the level. PAYDAY doubles the take and TURBO does not, by the rule the bumper kick already follows. The chips are drawn a pixel wider than anything a starfield ever scatters, and each is a block of stone with one lit corner and a shadow across from it that walk round it as it turns over. When the twenty-four seconds are up the fault heals back off the wall from the far column in, and the next kill is just a kill again.

  **PYRE turns your spare balls into ammunition.** Catching it tops the field up to three balls the way a first MULTI does, lights every one of them but the lowest with a **flame crown**, and for twenty seconds a click spends the ball nearest the wall: it goes up on the spot and takes every brick within two cells of it — thirteen of them, a plus five wide and five tall. **The last ball on the field can never be burned**, which is what the bare crown on the lowest ball says: that one is your reserve, and it changes hands the moment two balls cross, because who is nearest your deck is read every tick rather than decided at the catch. The ammunition comes with the capsule on purpose. Left to arm whatever the field happened to be carrying — and the field is carrying one ball most of the time — a capsule whose rule is that the last ball never burns is a capsule that does nothing at all when you catch it, which is the loudest way there is to ship a dud. So the twenty seconds are what the two newcomers are _for_: catch it and you are holding two grenades and the ball you started with, and you end where you began. The crater is measured in **cells and not pixels**, which is the only honest reading of "two bricks" on a grid of 30 × 12 ones — two brick widths is a sixty-pixel disc that takes forty bricks, two brick heights is a twenty-four-pixel one two columns wide — and the shockwave is drawn as the **ellipse those cells actually make**, running out to exactly the centres of the outermost bricks it took, so the ring you watch is the crater you got. Kills are of the nuke's class: full points, silver and gold in one, and no capsule drops, no BLAST splash, no CHAIN arc — thirteen bricks that each dropped a pill and set off their neighbours would make a spent ball worth more than the level, and the trade is a ball for a hole. **A click that frees GLUE's parked balls is spent on freeing them**: a player who has been clicking to serve since they caught the resin should never have the ball they were about to launch burned out from under them. The arrival is an ember wash rolling cap to cap across the deck, and the crowns will not take until it is half across; the ending is the same wash rolling back out and the crowns guttering into smoke **one after another** rather than all on one frame.

  **GIANT is the only capsule that changes the ball's hitbox, so everything about it follows from the size being real.** For sixteen seconds the ball swells from 8 px to 24 — three times across, and the same 24 px to the collision, because a ball that only looked bigger while colliding at 8 would read as a rendering fault rather than as a capsule. The sprite is **generated rather than blown up**: the eight rows the game has always drawn are the smallest case of a circle rasterised at whole pixels, so a giant ball is a crisp 24 px circle on the field's own pixel grid instead of a 3× zoom of a small one, and `ballRows(8)` reproduces the hand-authored table exactly — the ordinary ball is untouched to the pixel. The glint and the shade scale with it, which is what keeps a 24 px ball reading as the same sphere and not as a flat disc. **The swell is the transition, in the effect's own idiom**: 24 ticks each way, stepping the diameter two pixels at a time, growing **about the ball's centre** so a ball does not drift 16 px down and right as it arrives, and re-clamped inside the frame and back onto the deck if GLUE is holding it. What it does to the wall is the weight: a 24 px ball genuinely stands on **up to six cells** of a 30 × 12 grid, so it breaks all of them rather than the one the bounce test happened to sample first, and the ring of cells around that patch takes a splash hit on top — a mass this size does not stop at what it touched. The ring comes in as `"splash"` exactly as BLAST's does, so one contact stays one event: no chaining out of it, no capsules out of it, no eight overlapping pops. **It is gated on the ball's own size and not on the timer**, so the crush fades out with the swell over the same 24 ticks rather than switching off while the ball is still visibly heavy. A MULTI or SWARM fan thrown while it is live is born at full size, and it still bounces — a ball that ploughed straight on would be PIERCE, and the two are different capsules.

  **TRACER is the only capsule whose whole job is to be believed, so almost all of it is about refusing to guess.** For twenty seconds a ball on its way down pays out a dotted thread to the deck rail and pins a pip on the spot it will actually land — the real heading with the side-wall bounces walked in, not a plumb line under the ball. It changes nothing about the ball: it is XRAY's kind of capsule, information rather than physics, and the empty band between the wall and the deck is the ground it exists to cover, because that is where a ball is lost and where a player still has time to move. **A guide that lies once is worse than no guide at all**, since the player then stops believing the next one — so the thread draws only as far as it can promise and otherwise pins nothing. HOMING, ENGLISH, HAYWIRE and the two wells all bend a heading in flight, so while any of them is up the thread is a stub and the pip is gone; live bricks and bumper discs end it at the face it met; PORTAL is the good case, where the side walls stop reflecting and the thread runs through one doorway and out of the other. A ball with nothing to predict — climbing, or being bent — **hangs the thread visibly slack** instead, because a capsule that draws nothing for twenty seconds reads as one that broke, and slack against taut is a shape rather than a brightness. Only the ball arriving first draws its whole thread; every other falling ball pins its pip and no line, since twelve threads under SWARM is a cat's cradle and twelve pips on a rail is a reading. **The prediction replays the engine's own integration rather than solving for it**, and that is the part that makes the mark exact: `stepBall` does not mirror off a side wall, it clamps to the face and throws the overshoot away, so a closed-form reflection drifts up to 3.6 px per bounce — most of a ball, on the one pixel the player acts on. Walking it sub-step for sub-step the way the engine walks it puts the pip where the ball lands to 0.000 px, measured against the live game over 120 falls. The same walk is what the demo autopilot steers on, so the deck and the thread cannot disagree about where a ball is going. Under **SPLIT** the mark earns its keep twice: a pip landing inside the hole in the broken deck turns hazard pink and blinks, so the sign that means "be here" can also say "you cannot catch this from here".

  **Three capsules share one clock on the ball, and each of them shows it a different way.** `ballTimeScale()` is a single product of four blends — TEMPO's drift, RUSH's surge, TURBO's spool and STASIS's hold — so nothing is ever stored on a ball that would have to be unwound, and a field holding several of them composes instead of arbitrating. **RUSH's streak is its ease**: a smear is a distance, so the only honest way to ramp one is length, and over ten ticks the far crimson copy noses out of the back of the eight-pixel sprite before the amber one appears in the gap behind it — a sixth of a second, the shortest ease in the game, because RUSH is a trap made of suddenness. It retracts into the sprite the same way, and a TURBO underneath takes over the length without a jump. **TEMPO draws the difference it makes**: a pale outline of the ball marks where it would have been on the old clock, and the gap between the two _is_ the capsule's number. It is derived rather than simulated — a per-ball debt of the displacement the slowed clock never covered, spent forward along the stored velocity — because a phantom flying free walks through bricks and walls and, after one bounce, diverges in direction until the gap means nothing. So it dies the instant anything turns the ball, and stops where the field ends. It is an **outline and never a filled ball**: a filled sprite is the game's most loaded signal, and under a live SWARM twelve white ones would be TEMPO saying MULTI's sentence. **STASIS is its own release ring run backwards**: a twenty-pixel ring closes to two around every live ball over twelve ticks, riding it as it coasts, and the ball only brakes over the last six — the same twelve ticks and the same pixel-and-a-half a tick the release ring expands over, because two ring speeds would be the capsule telling two stories. The departure is left entirely to the ring pool, which pins one ring per ball where it hung; the closing ring is sent to the renderer one-way, since a symmetric blend would paint a second ring growing out of every ball on top of it.

  **HOMING's reticle flies in and closes on the brick.** The four corner ticks start four pixels out on the diagonal and walk in one whole pixel every three ticks until they sit on the corners — four discrete steps, because a 2×2 tick sliding on fractions would smear and a mechanism closing in stages is what a reticle is. Nothing is drawn at partial strength: the marks are full green from the first frame, they are simply somewhere else, and the distance they still have to travel is exactly how much steering the ball has yet. The travel is `homingRetargetTicks` to the tick, which is a constraint and not reuse — corners that took longer to shut than a lock is allowed to live would still be closing when their brick was re-chosen. **The reticle is per ball and the pull is global**: up to twelve balls hold up to twelve different bricks, so the marks ride a counter on the `Ball` while a single blend eases the turn rate, and the counter is restamped only when the lock genuinely changes cell — a lock re-taken on the clock, usually onto the same brick, must not close the corners afresh five times a second for the whole capsule. On expiry the corners let go, walk back out over the same twelve ticks and blink away over the last third, and it is each ball's own counter reaching zero that releases its lock rather than the expiry tick: the lock is the draw condition, which is why the marks used to vanish outright. `steerBall` stops the instant the timer dies rather than riding the blend out, because its first branch clears the lock on any ball below the grid heading down — which would blink that ball's corners off in the middle of their departure.

  **GLUE wets the deck, and the ball sticks to something you can see.** A film of amber resin creeps out from the middle of each half of the deck and beads along its top edge — the whole edge, cap to cap, not the sheen span, because that band is 28 px on a base deck but twelve under a JAMMER and **two** on each half of a SPLIT one, and a two-pixel cue is no cue. It spreads at a fixed **four pixels a tick** rather than over a fixed duration: a liquid does not know how wide the thing it is crossing is, so a 20 px SPLIT half finishes in three ticks and a 144 px XWIDE deck in eighteen, both visibly moving at the same speed. The front is ragged and the columns lagging furthest are the ones that bead, catching the row above the film line — a straight-edged film reads as a coloured rectangle, not as something wet. A ball that lands on it rides three pixels up on the resin with a two-pixel thread underneath, and both settle as the deck dries. The drying runs over the capsule's own last ticks and recedes at the same four a tick, so the last resin leaves on the last tick rather than the deck sitting dry-looking and still sticky for a quarter of a second. **The stick test itself never changes** — it is the one `isActive` branch it always was, because a deck that sometimes catches and sometimes bounces during its ramp is indistinguishable from a bug — and the release stays instant at the level's speed, since a bounce is instant and a released ball must not be briefly slower than a bounced one. A manual release snaps with no dry-down in front of it: that one is a deliberate action. MIRROR's ghost is wet too, through the same record, because the reflection is the paddle.

  **XRAY is a scan, so a bar crosses the wall.** What moves over the transition is **how far down the wall you can read, never how strongly**: a thin white-cyan line lies across the top of the grid on the catch and travels the full 360 px of its width down to the deepest live row over twenty ticks, three pixels a tick on a five-row wall and under five on the deepest one. Every brick it has passed shows its capsule pill at the measured `0.75`; the row it is crossing shows only the slice above the line, so a 12 px row develops over the three frames the bar spends on it, top edge downward, as if a plate were being exposed. A pill is whole, sliced, or not there — nothing ever fades. Ramping the reveal's alpha instead would be thirty pills breathing in together with no direction, at a glance the same event as GHOST's fade, and most of that ramp would be spent below the `0.65` where a pill sinks into its own brick, which is the exact range that constant exists to stay out of. The **live** wall sets the distance, sampled once as each sweep leaves its rest and held: normalised to the level's row count, a wall with one row left would be read in three ticks and the bar would crawl the other seventeen through empty space, degrading the transition back into a switch exactly when the wall is thinnest — and bricks die mid-sweep, so recomputing it per frame would jump the bar. The slice is a clip rect written in **device pixels by hand**, since this context is not pre-scaled, and it is branched rather than clipped blind: whole above the bar, cut across it, skipped below. On expiry the bar reappears at the bottom and retreats to the top, the deepest row losing its pill first and the top row last, and it is drawn only for the forty ticks the two sweeps take — a line parked on the wall for the two hundred and sixty in between stops being a scan and becomes a scratch on the glass. Presentational, with one honest cost: the bottom row's capsule is known twenty ticks later and forgotten twenty ticks earlier, and the top row's not at all.

  **QUAKE drops the wall instead of teleporting it a row.** The simulation's catch tick is unchanged — bottom row vaporised, rows shifted down, rattle started — but the wall is painted where it still stood and then falls the row it has just been given: twelve pixels on a squared curve, about three over the first five ticks and nine over the next five, accelerating into its landing. The rattle is still near 60 % of its amplitude when the wall arrives, so the two read as one event, and it goes quiet fourteen ticks later under a wall that has already settled rather than stopping on the same frame. Before this the wall was simply 12 px lower on the next frame and the shake was asked to cover a teleport it cannot cover: rattle is symmetric noise around zero and the jump is twelve pixels in one direction — noise conceals no displacement, it only makes the frame harder to point at. **The hitbox follows the paint rather than the index.** Pixel-to-cell happens in exactly one function, and that function is the whole of the wall's pixel-space collision — balls, laser bolts, meteors — so it takes one offset to keep them together; every other caller is already index-space and does not care. Left alone the played wall would sit twelve pixels below the drawn one, and a ball rising into the bottom row would bounce off nothing it could see, at the exact moment the screen is shaking and the player can least tell what cheated them. **One number says where the wall is this frame**, and everything drawn in wall coordinates reads it: the bricks, the capsule pills XRAY has revealed and the bar reading them, HOMING's reticle, CHAIN's arc, the death flashes, and the grub, whose row is falling out from under it. The `translate` is untouched — the shake moves the whole field, the drop moves only the wall, because the ground did not fall. There is no departure and none is owed: QUAKE is untimed, and the only thing that ends is the rattle.

  **PAYDAY gilds the wall from the deck up.** The capsule had no field cue at all: the score blinked and the POWER inset named it, but a player watching the wall could not tell it was live — and the wall is what pays. So a tide of gold runs up the brick sheen from the bottom row toward the top and drains back out through the bottom row when it stops. As the front passes a row, that row's one-pixel top sheen and lit left edge switch to the gild tone and **nothing else does**: kind colour, hurt state, dark shade and silhouette all stay, so a fully gilded wall still reads as the level it is. The tone is the gold brick's own `light` and not PAYDAY's roster colour, which is darker than every brick's highlight — painting a sheen with it would strip the highlight and leave the wall reading dirty and flat, the opposite of expensive — and which happens to _be_ the gold brick's flat, so on a hurt gold brick it would have been a literal no-op on exactly the levels a player expects PAYDAY to light up. Both branches of the sheen move, so the hurt tell survives one step down the same gold ramp instead of being flattened by the gild. **The kills carry the front too**: a brick killed behind it flashes gold rather than white, so mid-sweep the bottom rows are visibly already paying while the top rows still flash white, and the front is legible in the destruction as well as in the standing wall — decided when the brick dies, since a two-tick flash is a record of a kill and a tide moving on cannot make an earlier one gold. The boundary is hard where GHOST's is soft: that one cross-fades a whole brick, this swaps a single pixel row, and a half-gold pixel is a muddy pixel rather than a soft one. **The score blink waits for the tide to land and stops with the multiplier**, never one frame later — the flash is the effect at full strength and the wall is it arriving. The multiplier itself is untouched and always will be: a brick killed halfway through paying 1.5× would make the same run score differently depending on which tick landed where, and two runs that cannot be compared are not a hall of fame.

  **PORTAL cuts a door in the wall and pinches it shut.** A door, not a dissolve: the frame is painted first and the mouth over it, so the rows outside the aperture are simply not drawn and the thing closing the gap is the real wall rather than a tint of it. On the first frame it exists the side wall shows a two-pixel slit on the mouth's own centre line, and over twenty ticks it cuts open to the full forty-eight at a pixel and a fifth per lip — more than twice the speed the stripes inside it scroll at, so the aperture visibly outruns its own contents. The two travelling edges run white-hot while the cut is moving, and stop when it lands: a lip is where the cut has _reached_, and a door that has finished opening is not cutting anything. That white is the bumper rim's and not the portal's own bright blue, which is three of the nine stripe rows — a leading edge painted in it would vanish every time the scroll put a bright band under it, the tell blinking on and off at the stripe cycle's rate. **The door shuts inside its own capsule, not after it.** Driven off the timer expiring, the mouth would stay a live hitbox for twenty ticks after the panel had already dropped PORTAL — balls warping through a doorway the game had stopped admitting to, the first hitbox in the game to outlive its timer. So the close starts twenty ticks _before_ the end and the door is pinched shut on the exact frame the timer reaches zero. It costs almost nothing, and all of it at the edges: the mouth opens from the centre line, which is where transits actually happen, so what is missing at each end is the outer twenty-three pixels of a forty-eight-pixel door. **The aperture is the hitbox**, and one method owns both — the rows the renderer paints are the rows a ball is let through, by construction rather than by two formulas agreeing to the pixel, and the mouth's bottom edge is now exclusive so the hole is exactly the forty-eight rows that are drawn rather than the forty-nine it used to admit. The door also has a voice of its own now, a filtered rush rising as it opens and falling as it shuts, distinct from the transit's pitched warp by having no pitch at all.

  **BUMPERS energises disc by disc, and has its power cut.** A pinball table coming up, then losing the mains. For the first frames there is nothing on the field but a white ring twelve pixels out from a spot no disc occupies yet, closing onto it over twelve ticks and brightening as it tightens; the tick it lands the sprite is simply there, wearing the white eye it wears when it pays — so a disc's first frame is it kicking itself into existence. The rings start six ticks apart in the order the rack is laid, which is bottom-up, so the table lights from the deck upward and the player can count five, the last down at tick 36. **The departure is deliberately not staggered**: all five leave together on the expiry tick, each on one ring travelling back out on the kick flash's own alpha ramp. An arrival is a boot sequence and a power cut is not one — and it keeps the picture honest, because after the timer there is nothing left on screen a ball could have hit. The ring is the disc's own material and the sprite is never scaled: the radius is baked into two hand-tabled pixel row sets, and a scaled draw would resample pixel art the game deliberately does not resample. **A converging ring is a warning, not a surface**, so the kick skips any disc still on its way in or out — the arrival costs the first disc twelve ticks and the last thirty-six of a seven-hundred-and-twenty-tick life, and nobody loses a kick they had aimed. Two things fall out of it that were broken before. A second catch during those twelve departing ticks used to buy a live twelve-second timer over an empty field, because the spawn is skipped while the records still exist; now the discs the player is watching leave snap back with their eyes lit, which is a top-up said in the rack's own language. And the wedge guard — the streak limit that lets a ball go when it is trapped between two discs — used to cut the rack out of existence mid-substep without ever passing through the expiry branch; it now retires like everything else, so the ball is released on the same tick and the rack is _seen_ going.

  **The energy wall is written out of the deck, and spent where the ball struck it.** A charge, not a fade. On the catch frame the bar is two pixels under the paddle's own centre; over fifteen ticks it writes itself both ways at a constant twenty-four pixels a tick until each end runs into the frame. The deck is rarely centred, so the near end lands on its wall first and the far end runs on alone — the same 366-pixel bar every time, drawn differently every time, always drawn out of where the player happened to be standing. That constant speed and the single number behind it are the same thing: the field is exactly 366 pixels across, so a reach of `366 × blend` either side of the origin, clamped to the frame, is both. **The strike is the origin.** When a ball is saved, the two pixels it struck go white-hot for three ticks and both ends run inward at thirty-six a tick to be swallowed at that x — faster than the charge, because spending is not deliberate — so the last pixel to go out is the one directly under the ball it saved, and the player learns which bounce was the free one. Collapsing toward the middle of the field was rejected: nothing happened there. Two things this had to get right. Re-arming mid-collapse would tear the bar sideways to the deck and re-expand it from somewhere else, so the origin moves only when there is no bar on screen to move; a second WALL caught during those ten ticks simply restarts the charge from where the bar already is. And the picture is never allowed to lie about the reach: the save arms across the whole line from the catch frame and has to, because WALL's signature moment is catching it with a ball already past the deck and refusing that would be a life taken by an animation — so when a strike lands outside what the bar had written, **the bar completes itself in the instant it pays**, which is what a barrier catching a ball at a point it had not reached should look like. None of it decides anything: the save is `wallArmed` and always was. The arm has a voice now too, a short rising pair under a filtered breath, which the game's one free life had been arriving without.

  **MAGNET's reach opens out of the rail, ninety-six pixels you can see.** What arrives and leaves is neither the tether nor the pull strength but the **range**. Ninety-six pixels of it open out of the deck over twenty ticks at nearly five a tick — almost four times the speed a capsule falls, so the edge overtakes the field rather than crawling after it — taking hold of each falling capsule as it sweeps past, in order of distance, each tether appearing at the moment the edge reaches its capsule instead of five appearing at once. On expiry it closes back into the deck, letting go of the far ones first. Fading the tethers was rejected: the tether is the symptom and the range is the effect. Ramping the strength was rejected harder — a magnet that warms up everywhere would feel unreliable right next to the deck, which is where catches are decided, so the pull is at full power the instant the edge takes hold and only the arm gets longer. **The transition needed a body of its own or it would usually be invisible**: the capsule a MAGNET catch guarantees is guaranteed on the _next_ kill, which happens whenever a brick next dies and then falls for a hundred ticks before it means anything, so the reach routinely opens across an empty field. Two small marks on the rail now sit at the band's edges and split out of the deck as it opens — they are the first thing the game has ever drawn that says the magnet is a horizontal band and not a radius, and they are skipped rather than pinned when the band runs off the field, because a mark on the wall would claim the reach ends there. **One number is the band**: the pull's gate, the cut the tethers are drawn to, and the marks all read the same reach, so the picture cannot promise a hold the simulation is not making. It eases the simulation and that is the whole idea — the config already says the magnet "biases a capsule toward the paddle without ever promising it", so a ramp at each end bites only at the extreme edge, on the weakest pull the effect has, exactly where it already promises nothing. One latent crash went with it: at zero reach a capsule sitting dead on the deck's centre line divided nothing by nothing and had its x set to NaN, which is a capsule that is nowhere for the rest of its life.

  **The grub walks on and dies; the rock is used up.** Two things on the field that used to appear and vanish at full size, and neither wanted a blend — both already own a step and a record, so the transition is a counter on the object and motion the object performs itself. **CRITTER is an animal, so it arrives**: the grub is put down a full sprite-width outside the field, behind the bar the wall is painted with, and walks in at its ordinary chewing pace over about eight of its nine hundred ticks, the red jaw emerging from under the bar first because the jaw is drawn on the leading end. It takes one flag: the wrap-and-turn branch that drops the grub a row when it runs out of wall would otherwise fire on the very first tick of the walk-in, clamping it back onto the grid, flipping it around and dropping it a row before the player had seen it. And it dies visibly — the stride clock halves so the feet drag, the lime body steps twice toward its own belly brown, then it blinks out in two-tick blocks before the yellow puff. The colour moves in **two discrete steps and not a blend**, because a two-colour ten-by-eight sprite has no in-between and a lerp on it would only dither; under DEMAKE all three tones flatten to one ink, which is why the drag and the blink carry the tell as well. **It had to answer both exits or it would be right half the time.** The life timer is only one of them: the config's own comment says the grub "usually walks off the bottom first", and that exit used to pop it out of existence on the boundary frame with no warning at all. So one number counts down to whichever end is coming — the timer, or the travel left before it runs off the bottom row — and the walk-off is a real walk-off now: it declines the turn there is no row for, keeps going the way it was headed and leaves under the far bar, the entrance run backwards, with no puff, because a grub the player watched leave owes nobody an explosion. The travel is measured at the chewing pace rather than the current one, so the clock only ever falls and the run-down cannot start and stop again as the grub crosses ground it has stripped; over cleared ground it arrives at double that pace and the whole run-down plays in half the ticks, which is right — it really is leaving twice as soon. **METEOR's rock is used up rather than deleted.** Passing the bottom of the wall stops removing it: the ember cap goes out, then the white-hot core steps down from four pixels to one over twelve ticks, three ticks a rung, still falling and still drifting along the line it was launched on. While it burns it puffs every tick instead of every other, so the rock turns into its own smoke — shrinking at exactly the rate the trail behind it thickens — and twelve is the trail chunk's own shortest life, so the last puff a rock makes outlives the rock. It is armed as a **one-shot**, because being past the bottom is true on every tick after the first and a naive set-then-decrement would re-arm forever and the rock would never die at all. It drills nothing new — below the last row there is no cell to find — but it does hold its slot in the pool twelve ticks longer, and METEOR stays lit in the POWER inset for those ticks, which is right: a rock is still on screen.

  **1UP racks a paddle rather than rebuilding the rack.** A life in this game is a paddle in reserve, so the new one is delivered to the rack — and the reason it could not be before is the same reason the panel had a bug: the LIVES readout called `replaceChildren` with a whole fresh array, so adding one bar destroyed and rebuilt every bar beside it in the same frame. Nothing can be animated onto a node that is thrown away each time the number changes, so the fix and the transition are one change. The rack is diffed now, appended to and trimmed from its end, so exactly one node is new — and it grows out of its own left edge over three hundred milliseconds while the mint the catalogue outlines its new deck in fades off it. It is the last child, so nothing reflows and no bar beside it moves; death gets the fix for free at the same line, since losing a bar stops being a full-rack teardown too. **The two properties it animates are not a free choice.** The stage transitions `color`, `background-color`, `border-color` and `text-shadow` for DEMAKE's half-second sag, and a life bar's fill is `background`, which the shorthand routes through `background-color` — so growing the fill or a border would be eased twice over, once by the animation and once by the dissolve. `transform` and `outline-color` are outside that list, which is exactly why the growth is a `scaleX` and the mark is an outline; one pixel at one pixel of offset, because the rack is a three-pixel flex gap around a five-pixel bar and anything wider reaches into the neighbouring bar and over the label above it. The mint carries a DEMAKE override of its own, so the one thing that mode exists to forbid — a colour glowing on a monochrome screen — does not happen here. **The new bar is marked because a 1UP was caught, not because the number went up**, which is the same number for two different reasons: the first render appends both starting bars at once, and a restart out of GAME OVER can add exactly one bar to whatever the last death left behind, both of which would otherwise mint-pulse as though the player had caught something. So the game counts the lives it grants and the panel watches that count. **And the refusal has a voice.** Caught on a full rack the clamp bit, the count never changed, the panel short-circuited, and the capsule was a complete no-op apart from the pop — silence where the game had something to say. "The rack is full" is a different sentence from "a life arrived", so it is said differently: a mint pulse across the whole rack rather than on one bar, because what is full is the rack. It rides a monotonic count rather than a flag, since the panel view is compared field by field and a boolean is true for exactly one frame and can never be said twice, and the pulse is restarted by dropping the class, forcing a reflow and putting it back — without that the second refusal in a row goes quiet again, exactly when the player is trying the capsule to see what it does. There is no departure to design and none is owed: 1UP is untimed, activates no timer, and what it grants is a counter that persists until a death spends it. The three hundred milliseconds are a derivation and not a knob — the catch pop lives forty-eight ticks and starts blinking the word apart at sixteen left, so the bar lands fourteen ticks before 1UP breaks up and the two read as one event.

  **RAIN falls in through the ceiling.** It used to appear inside the field: four opaque full-size pills existed at eight pixels below the frame on a frame where nothing had been, with no flash or shatter to cover it — the one capsule whose whole content is capsules, arriving by materialising them. The birth moves above the ceiling now, a pill-height clear of the frame plus a one-sided scatter, and **the fall the object already performs is the entire transition**: at 1.3 pixels a tick each pill wipes into view a row at a time from under the three-pixel band the frame is painted with, over six ticks and up to eighteen with the scatter. That band is laid down after both capsule-drawing paths, the ordinary one and BLACKOUT's, so the pill is covered by the torchlit frame too. No blend, no view field, no renderer change and no new state on the capsule — and the number is a distance over a speed rather than a fade counter, which is the point: a rained capsule now enters the field on exactly the physics a brick-dropped one does. Scaling or fading a pill into existence was rejected for the same reason — a capsule's canonical entrance is falling out of a dead brick, and a second magical way to come into being is one object with two physics. The scatter is deliberately **one-sided** where the horizontal jitter beside it is symmetric: a lane can be off either way, but a capsule may only ever start higher, never lower, or the entrance it exists to give back gets skipped. **This is the one change in the epic a player feels in the hand**, and it was taken on purpose: four capsules crossing the deck line on the same frame spread over 366 pixels is an uncatchable set by construction, so RAIN promised four and delivered one or two — the shower now lands a fifth of a second later and arrives as weather, in an order the player can actually work. One guard came with it, in another file: MAGNET's tethers culled on nothing but the capsule being live and inside the horizontal band, so a pill still above the frame would have been strung to the deck by a line running down to a head nobody could see. The bound is the pill's bottom edge against the ceiling, so the tether appears on the very frame the pill does. The pull itself is **not** gated with it — the capsule really is inside the band, and this culls what can be drawn rather than what the magnet is holding; gated too, a pill would clear the frame on its launch line and only then start sliding, which is the magnet visibly picking it up late. And `spawnAcrossTop` is shared with the dev console on purpose, so a capsule asked for by hand now enters through the ceiling as well, which is what its own comment always wanted.

  **A trap says so before you catch it**: its glyph blinks as it falls, and the catch pop is pink with a detuning womp instead of the usual chime. That tell is the `trap` tier itself, so it costs a new trap capsule nothing to get all three.

  **DEMAKE is the one trap that costs nothing but nerve.** For 8 seconds the whole machine downgrades itself — playfield, side panel and sound chip drop to a 1-bit green phosphor tube with scanlines, flat squares and no noise channel — while the simulation underneath is untouched: same ball, same paddle, same score. It sags into the tube and back out over half a second at each end rather than flipping, so it reads as hardware giving up and not as a dropped frame: the canvas paints both machines and crossfades them, the panel rides a CSS transition over the same tokens, and the chip — which cannot dissolve — gives out as the picture passes halfway. It is one of the three capsules that touch presentation and nothing else — BLACKOUT and FLIP are the others — and the only one a run is **guaranteed** to meet: every first level has a DEMAKE seeded into it, in a random cell of the third, fourth or fifth row from the bottom. Never the front two, because the gag only reads as the machine breaking down once you have seen the machine working, and those rows go in the opening rally. It stays barred from that level's rolls, so the one you meet is the one the wall was built around, and the seed costs the bag nothing — a pinned capsule is not a draw, so the odds everywhere after level 1 are exactly what they were. The reduction runs off two tones and one rule — the sprite palette's _shadow_ role becomes the tube's ground and everything else its ink, so the 1px bevels the art is banded with survive and the wall stays a wall; the field theme is thresholded by luma into the same two tones and cached beside its colour twin.

  **BLACKOUT is the second, and it is the cheaper trick.** For 20 seconds the lights go out: the field is black but for a pool of light travelling with each ball and a dimmer one on the deck. Nothing in the simulation is told — same ball, same paddle, same score — and the capsules stay lit as they fall, deliberately, since a trap caught because it could not be read would be the trap punishing you twice. More balls light more of the field: each extra live ball takes 6 px off every pool, from 58 solo down to a floor of 26, so MULTI and SWARM buy visibility without ever lifting the trap. A NUKE's sweep and the last brick's shatter light the field by definition, and since neither freeze runs the timers, the dark gets its seconds back afterwards. The veil is one canvas built at field size and blitted up with smoothing off, with a radial gradient per ball punched out of it — so the falloff steps in whole game pixels, which is the art everything else on the field is drawn in. It does not switch on: the pools open wider than the field and **close onto the ball over three quarters of a second**, then run the same way back out when the capsule expires — a light dropping fast and then dying slowly, which is what the power-down on the catch sounds like. Nothing cross-fades; the dark is never painted at half strength, it simply has not reached you yet.

  **FLIP is the third, and the only one that touches your hands.** For 8 seconds the arena turns over — the wall at the bottom, the deck riding the ceiling, the frame's closed edge drawn where the bricks now are so you can see which side still kills you — and the mouse turns with it, so the deck on screen keeps following the hand. Nothing under it moves: the same ball falls the same way in simulation space, and losing it is still the bottom of the field, which is now the top of the screen. It does not snap over: it **rotates about the field centre over half a second** at each end, shrinking just enough on the way round for a 372×300 field standing on its side to still fit a 300 px canvas, and settling on an exact point reflection where every sprite lands back on whole device pixels. The field art stays put underneath, for the same reason it does not ride QUAKE's shake — it is the room, not something standing on the field. The mouse changes over at the halfway point rather than continuously, because at a quarter turn the field is on its side and there is no left or right to lend a hand. Two things stay the right way up: the letter on a falling capsule and the catch pop, both counter-rotated about their own centre off the canvas matrix itself, so a trap caught upside down can still be read as one.

  **COMBOS are what two capsules do together, and nothing falls to make one.** Six authored pairs fuse into a third effect for as long as both halves are live, announced by one chord and written in the POWER inset after the halves that made them: **LANCE** (LASER+PIERCE) stops the shot dying on its first brick, so one shot rips a column; **NOVA** (PIERCE+BLAST) widens the splash from the eight neighbours to the 5×5 block, 24 cells; **CHARGE** (GLUE+LASER) holds the cannons while the deck is holding a ball and spends the fire as a salvo on the release — on a real release, never on an idle click; **STROBE** (LASER+TEMPO) halves the cadence, so the guns keep real time while the balls run at 0.6; **JACKPOT** (BLAST+PAYDAY) doubles what a splashed brick pays on top of PAYDAY's own double, ×12 with TURBO over it; **OVERTIME** (TEMPO+PAYDAY) stops PAYDAY's clock for the length of TEMPO's, so twenty seconds of money can run to thirty-six. The table is authored and stays authored — inventing a capsule invents no fusion — and a combo dies the moment either half expires, in silence, because the half that ran out is the event and it has its own tell. Combo glyphs are derived the way capsule glyphs are but against the capsule names _and_ the combo ones (`LAN`, `NO`, `CHAR`, `STR`, `JAC`, `OV`), so a fusion can never wear a pill the player has already learned.

  **FUSE is the one pill that means "the missing half."** Left to overlap on their own, combos are rare by accident — the mechanic asks two specific timers to coincide and nothing arranges that, so a whole run can go by without one. FUSE arranges it: catch it holding PIERCE and BLAST arrives, so NOVA lights on the spot; catch it holding nothing useful and a whole pair lands together. It never fakes a fusion — it grants the real capsules, each at full duration, and the detection above lights the combo by itself. That is also why it **tops the half you already had back to full**: a PIERCE with half a second left would otherwise light NOVA for half a second and read as a broken capsule. Holding all six fusions at once, it tops a pair up and says nothing, because nothing new formed.

  The side panel's **POWER** inset names the live effects while they fit its 13 characters (`WIDE MULTI x3`) and packs them into a still glyph row when they do not (`E M3 L P B +5`) — a row that holds still can be read mid-rally, which a label cycling one name per second could not.

- **Scoring**: 60–200 points per brick by kind, level-clear bonus `(level+1) × 500`. The top-5 hall of fame is **shared across all players**: scores live in a server-side SQLite table behind the `shatter-api` service, with `localStorage` (`shatter.hiscores.v1`) as instant-boot cache and offline fallback — the game never waits on the network.
- **Audio**: WebAudio chiptune SFX, 100% synthesized (no assets): a per-event sound bank (square pitch-bends, detuned pairs, filtered noise bursts) on a master gain → compressor chain, with a 30 ms retrigger guard against same-tick pile-ups. An SFX volume fader lives in the side panel (persisted in `localStorage` `shatter.volume.v1`, scaling the master gain ahead of the compressor). An inaudible 30 Hz keep-warm tone stops browser/HDMI/Bluetooth silence detection from swallowing short impact blips. Every voice in the bank goes out through three private wrappers, which is what lets DEMAKE downgrade the chip along with the screen in one flag rather than one branch per sound: glides, detuning and the noise channel all go, leaving flat squares.

### Controls

- **Mouse** moves the paddle; **click** or **Space** starts the game, launches the ball, and advances screens.
- Clicking also engages **Pointer Lock** (relative `movementX` control); without lock, absolute pointer position is used. Press `Esc` once to exit lock; `Esc` also closes the LEVELS gallery and the CAPSULES catalogue back to the title.
- **P** pause · **M** sound on/off · **L** levels gallery · **B** capsules catalogue (both from the title) · **VOL** fader in the side panel (mouse-only, reachable whenever the cursor is free — title, pause, before launch) · **ESC** quit run (from lock, press `Esc` twice: first exits lock, second quits), or leave the levels gallery and the capsules catalogue.
- The mouse is the only paddle control, so a run **auto-pauses** whenever it would go on unsteered: cursor leaving the window, window losing focus, or pointer lock dropping. Click to resume — that click also re-engages the lock.

Test gameplay quickly with the **test console** — `Ctrl`+`Option`+`Command`+`K` (⌃⌥⌘K) during serve, play or pause, the same screens WARP allows. It opens a modal over the field, in the pause screen's own style, and freezes the run behind it. The modal lists its own commands and every capsule, and `Enter` applies the line and closes. **Every command is a word followed by its arguments** — `POWER MU`, not `MU`. A line it cannot use keeps what you typed and says why, so a mistake is one `Backspace` from fixed: a bare capsule or number answers `TYPE: POWER MU` / `TYPE: LEVEL 3` / `TYPE: BONUS 0.5`, a known command with a bad argument answers for itself (`LEVELS START AT 1`, `BONUS IS 0 TO 1`, `NO SUCH CAPSULE: ZZ`, `POWER NEEDS A CAPSULE`), and only an unrecognised word falls back to `UNKNOWN COMMAND`:

- `power nuke` · `power NU` · `power WI MU LA` — **make capsules fall**, spread across the top of the field, to be caught with the paddle like any other. It does not grant the effect: the console freezes the run, so anything applied outright had already happened by the time you were looking at the game again, and the effect _arriving_ — which is usually the thing you opened the console to watch — was the one thing you could never see. Nothing happens while the modal is up; they drop on the first live tick after it closes. Miss one and it is gone, which is two words and a chord to fix. **Name or glyph, whichever you remember** (and the internal id still answers too) — and the modal prints the whole roster (`WI WIDE`, `MU MULTI`, …) underneath, generated from the capsule registry, so a new capsule appears there by itself. Repeat one for several (`power MU MU MU`); one unrecognised capsule refuses the whole line, and so does a line the drop pool has no room for (`NO ROOM · CAPSULES ALREADY FALLING`) rather than spawning half of it.
- `level 12` — jump to a level (1-based, unbounded: runs loop past level 35, so `level 37` is level 2 at its wrapped ball speed)
- `bonus 1` — set `bonusSpreadAmount` for this run: the chance a destroyed brick drops a capsule at all, not how fast one falls (0..1; kept until changed or reloaded)
- `gamble nuke` — pin what GAMBLE's reel lands on, so a one-in-forty-three result can be tested without catching capsules until it comes up; `gamble` on its own hands it back to chance. It resolves a name, a glyph or an id like `power` does, and refuses anything the reel cannot land on (`NOT ON THE REEL: JAMMER`), asked of the very list the reel rolls from, so a pin can never produce a result the game cannot reach. Unlike the other three this is a setting, not an action, so it survives a lost ball and a new run — a testing tool you would otherwise retype after every mistake.

**It gives the cursor back, and it never traps you.** Opening the console releases pointer lock first: the cursor reappears where you can see it, and `Escape` reaches the page — the browser only swallows that key when it needs it to exit a lock, which is why a modal must not hold one. Losing the lock also pauses a live run, which is exactly the freeze wanted behind a modal. `Escape`, the chord again, or a click anywhere all close it; the click that resumes from the pause screen re-captures the cursor as usual.

The console is **dev-always, production-on-request**: it is built behind `import.meta.env.DEV` **or** the `gameConfig.rules.testConsole` knob below. With the knob down, `src/core/DevConsole.ts` and the chord that opens it are absent from production bundles, exactly like the `?level=` / `?droprate=` / `?power=` URL params it replaces. It builds its own markup and carries its own inline styles for the same reason: nothing about it reaches `index.html` or `css/`.

### The chord

Both debug chords — the console's and WARP's — are `Ctrl` + `Alt` held with **a third modifier that is either `Command` or `Shift`**:

|                    | Console                              | Skip the level                       |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| **Mac**            | `Ctrl`+`Option`+`Command`+`K` (⌃⌥⌘K) | `Ctrl`+`Option`+`Command`+`N` (⌃⌥⌘N) |
| **Mac or Windows** | `Ctrl`+`Alt`+`Shift`+`K`             | `Ctrl`+`Alt`+`Shift`+`N`             |

Command was the Mac answer on its own: nothing claims all three there, since Chrome binds ⌘N and ⇧⌘N only and macOS has no ⌃⌥⌘ default. On Windows the same `event.metaKey` is the **Windows key**, and `Ctrl`+`Alt`+`Win`+ a letter is both a knuckle-breaker and the OS's to claim — so `Shift` stands in for it, and `Ctrl`+`Alt`+`Shift`+ a letter is free in Chrome there. Either modifier is accepted rather than one per platform sniffed from the user agent: a Mac keeps the chord its owner already has in their fingers, and a browser lying about its platform cannot take the shortcut away from anyone. `Ctrl`+`Alt`+`Shift` also works on the Mac.

Three debug affordances work in **production builds too**:

- **WARP easter egg** — the chord over `N` during serve, play or pause instantly wins the current level, straight to the CLEARED screen. Matched on the physical key (`event.code === "KeyN"`), because Option rewrites `event.key` into the alternate glyph while the N keycap sits in the same place on AZERTY, QWERTY and QWERTZ. **A warp scores nothing** — no brick points, and the clear bonus shows `00000`: the hall of fame is shared across all players, so skipping a level must never be worth points.
- **The console knob** — `gameConfig.rules.testConsole` (`src/core/config/GameConfig.ts`) ships the console to players, chord and all: that is how a machine which is not the developer's gets to try a capsule on the live site. `false` is the resting value, and putting it back is one line and a redeploy. While it is `true` the console is in the public bundle, so anyone who finds the chord has it — and unlike WARP, which scores nothing, a run helped along by `power` posts a real score to the shared hall of fame. `deploy.sh` prints the value in the deploy log.
- **The bonus knob** — `gameConfig.rules.bonusSpreadAmount` (`src/core/config/GameConfig.ts`) is the chance a destroyed brick drops a capsule: crank it to `1` while debugging (every brick drops one), set it back to what players should get before deploying. It ships as-is; `deploy.sh` prints the value in the deploy log so a knob left cranked is caught by eye. In dev, the console's `bonus` command overrides it for one run without touching the file.

### Engine details

- **Fixed timestep**: 60 Hz accumulator (frame delta clamped to 50 ms, max 4 catch-up steps per frame); rendering every animation frame.
- **Sub-stepped ball movement**: each tick is split into `ceil(max(|vx|,|vy|)/2)` micro-steps, X then Y, with a brick-grid collision query per axis — fast balls can't tunnel through bricks.
- **Brick death effects**: destroyed bricks flash white and burst into debris from a 1024-slot particle ring buffer; the NUKE sweep and the level-clear delay freeze the simulation (only effects tick), so the clear screen never cuts an animation short and no ball can be lost behind an explosion.
- **Brick collision**: O(1) grid lookup (`cellAt`) + 4-corner overlap test for the 8px ball, instead of scanning all bricks.
- **Paddle bounce**: `relativeHit ∈ [-1, 1]` → angle `relativeHit × 1.05 rad`; speed is preserved, so center hits go up and edge hits go wide.
- **Ball speed**: `min(4.6, 3.1 + level × 0.25)` px/tick, times the `ballSpeedMultiplier` config.
- **Canvas palette** lives in `src/render/palette.ts` (canvas cannot read CSS custom properties); the same colors are exposed to the DOM as CSS tokens in `css/tokens/colors.css`.
- **Capsules are a registry**: `src/core/config/powerUps.ts` holds one row per power-up — id, glyph, name, body color, letter tone, duration, rarity tier, timed-ness — and everything else derives from it: the `PowerUpKind` union, the name/glyph/duration/ticket lookups, `MALUS_KINDS`, `DROP_COLORS` and `DARK_LETTER_DROP_KINDS` in the palette, the timers' countdown list, and the console's roster. **Rarity is a tier, and a tier is a count of tickets in a shuffled bag**: two for a `common`, one for `uncommon`, `rare` and `trap` alike, so a pass is 74 tickets inside which every capsule falls once or twice, and no capsule can go missing for longer than two passes. It used to be four weights (`common` 1, `uncommon` 0.6, `trap` 0.7, `rare` 0.35) rolled afresh per drop, and the roll is what had to go: a 2.9× spread across 48 rows still left every capsule absent from most levels, so no number in that table was ever going to be the fix. Drawing without replacement was. **Two of the three old weight exceptions survive as ticket counts** — DEMAKE and GIANT draw at a common's two, because both already did so under weights and a rare's single ticket would have been worse over the first four levels, which is the horizon a session actually reaches; VORTEX's is retired as pure arithmetic. `check:drops` holds both the guarantee and those two rows. The `trap` tier doubles as the malus flag, driving the blinking letter, the pink catch pop and the womp. **Adding a capsule is adding one row**, and forgetting to is a type error rather than a silent gap. The **glyph is separate from the id** because one letter stopped meaning anything around the fifteenth capsule — three names open on B and three on P, and a lone `B` said nothing about which. A pill shows **the first two letters of the name**, and more only where two names collide (MIRROR and MIMIC would both be `MI`, so they become `MIR`/`MIM`; BLAST and BLACKOUT still tie at three, so they become `BLAS`/`BLAC`). That is derived from the names, not authored per row, because it is a property of the whole roster: adding a capsule can lengthen an old one's glyph in the same breath. Ids stay as they are — they are internal, and thirty-odd `kind === "..."` branches are written in them. The pill's font is picked by measuring the glyph against the sheen span rather than by counting characters (7 → 5 → 4px), so a longer name costs a font rung and never overflows — Silkscreen is proportional, and two letters turn out to keep the 7px the roster drew single letters at. In dev, a pass after `document.fonts.ready` measures every glyph against the pill, checks no two capsules ended up with the same one, and checks every letter clears 3:1 on its body.
- **Backgrounds are pre-rendered**: each level names a theme (`background` in its definition) painted once into an offscreen 1× layer and blitted per frame with smoothing off (an exact 3× upscale), so theme detail is free in the loop — measured slightly cheaper than the old flat fill + 58 star rects. Layouts come from a seeded generator keyed by theme and level, so a level's field art never changes between visits. Theme tones are split into `area` (large regions, kept as dark as the classic field) and `speck` (1–3px sparkle); `pnpm run check:backgrounds` fails the build on a tone that is too bright, too close to a brick/capsule color, or on two adjacent levels sharing a theme.
- **Panel updates are diffed**: DOM text is only written when a value changes, never per frame.
- **Stage scaling**: `transform: scale(min(0.99·vw/480, 0.99·vh/300))`, with the stage rect cached and invalidated on resize/scroll; pointer coordinates are mapped through the scale.

### Score API

- `GET /api/scores` → `{ scores: [{ name, score }] }` (top 15, ties rank by insertion order).
- `POST /api/scores` `{ name, score }` → `201` with the fresh top 15, or `422` on invalid input.
- Server-side validation (the client is not trusted): names must match `^[A-Z0-9]{3}$` after uppercasing, a small blocklist of crude combos lands as `???`, scores are integers `0..10 000 000`.
- Per-IP in-memory rate limit (6 POST/min, `429`) behind the nginx `limit_req` zone; submitter IPs are stored for abuse cleanup.
- SQLite file: `server/data/shatter.db` locally (gitignored), `/home/debian/apps/shatter-api/data/shatter.db` on ks-b — a brand-new table is seeded with the classic board (AMI, CBM, PAL, FDD, KIK, AGA, ECS, OCS, DMA, CIA, SID, C64, MOD, WB1, RAM).
- The front (`ScoreApi`) treats every failure as `null` and keeps the `localStorage` table; a remote sync landing refreshes whichever screen currently shows scores.

### Deployment

- Target host default: `debian@ks-b`, app folder `/var/www/shatter` (URL `https://shatter.1991computer.com/`). Everything lives inside it, bkmk-style: live root `front/` (the nginx root), versioned history `front-releases/`, previous version `front.bak/`.
- Nginx vhost: `/etc/nginx/conf.d/shatter.conf` on ks-b, with its own Let's Encrypt certificate (webroot renewal, like the other subdomains). The legacy `https://1991computer.com/arkanoid-2007/` path is dropped and returns 404.
- Each deploy creates a versioned `front-releases/release-<timestamp>-<branch>-<hash>` — the folder name carries the metadata; previous live version is kept as `front.bak` and restored automatically if the healthcheck fails. What a deploy shipped is recorded in Zeus, not in the build: the report carries release, commit, branch, status and the commit range.
- Healthcheck marker in the deployed HTML: `SHATTER`.
- Overridable via env vars (`REMOTE_USER_HOST`, `WEB_ROOT_BASE`, `HEALTHCHECK_URL`, `EXPECTED_HTML_MARKER`, `MAX_RELEASES_TO_KEEP`, `BUILD_BASE_PATH`).
- The score API deploys separately with `./scripts/deploy-api.sh`: rsync of `server/` to `/home/debian/apps/shatter-api` (the `data/` directory is never touched), `pnpm install --prod --frozen-lockfile`, `pm2 startOrReload`, then a healthcheck through the nginx `/api/` proxy.
- The nginx `/api/` location must set `proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-For $remote_addr;` (overwrite, not append) — the API trusts exactly that one hop (`trustProxy: "127.0.0.1"`) so clients cannot forge `request.ip` to dodge the rate limit.

## Explanation

The 2007 original (and its first modernization) rendered the ball, paddle, and bricks as DOM elements. REV 2.0 moves gameplay rendering to a single pixel-art canvas — DOM stays where it is better (crisp text in the side panel and overlay screens) and the canvas handles everything that moves at 60 Hz. The Claude Design mockup is the spec of record: its embedded script defines the exact geometry, physics constants, palette, and screen flow that this codebase re-implements as typed, testable modules.
